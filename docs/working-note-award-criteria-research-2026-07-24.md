## 1. Scorecard against the five criteria

The five review criteria as written (verified against the `overview/evaluation` page text): Technical clarity and reproducibility / Methodological contribution / Security insight / Usefulness to the benchmark community / Responsible communication.

### 1.1 Technical clarity and reproducibility

**Current state**: the structural evidence is strong — nearly every claim is anchored to a specific file:line, §5.2 is a numbered 5-step formal proof ending in "∎", Appendix B gives a 6-step executable reproduction recipe, and Appendix C pins the source code to specific commits and discloses the public PyPI package version. But "reproducible" rests entirely on "the reader manually runs the third-party SDK code we point them to"; **none of our own generation-side code (the rung generator, the multi-post forgery template, the `_fill()` fill logic) has any runnable artifact linked out** (grepping this repo for our own GitHub/Kaggle-code/Colab links returns zero hits).

**Research support**: all 5 award-winning writeups of the analog competition linked an external runnable artifact (one of GitHub/Colab/HF Space/Kaggle Dataset); Kaggle's official Solution Write-Up rubric lists "detailed enough that any data scientist could reproduce it" as a separate scored item, Strong Supporting Materials.

### 1.2 Methodological contribution

**Current state**: currently the Note's strongest dimension — §9 distills the finding into a general, falsifiable structural proposition ("guardrail window ⊇ scorer window ⇒ that class of failure is unscoreable") with a formal proof, not a one-off exploit record; the throughput cost model in §6 is cross-validated by three independent public working notes in §7.2 via different routes. But the dialogue with the existing provenance-defense literature (StruQ/CaMeL/secure-agent-design-patterns) is very thin — Appendix C.2 dispatches it in one sentence, without arguing the specific relationship between the window-nesting failure mode and those existing defense models.

**Research support**: the analog competition's ARC Prize six-dimension scoring table lists Novelty (novelty relative to published research) and Theory (why it works, not just how) as separate scored items — "methodological contribution" is not something you can dispatch in a general wave of the hand; it gets broken out and scored individually.

### 1.3 Security insight

**Current state**: the guardrail/scorer misalignment for this specific benchmark is explained deeply and accurately, and the scope is repeatedly, explicitly narrowed to "one deliberately vulnerable public benchmark," not extended to real deployed systems — the scope discipline itself is a plus for responsible disclosure, but it also self-limits the value boundary of this "security insight" dimension fairly narrowly, and it is easy to read as "a bug report inside a challenge sandbox" rather than "a finding of general significance for real agent security." The abstract pattern in §9 is never named into a general security-pattern category a reader would recognize (e.g., check-then-use timing misalignment / TOCTOU-style structure), and it does not explain why real production systems (which commonly also split a fast synchronous filter from a slower independent audit layer) might share the same structural defect.

**Research support**: the Limitations section of the analog competition's *Mind the Gap* explicitly delimits its own method's generalization boundary ("AgentSeer is tied to LangGraph and MLFlow, limiting framework generality"); ARC Prize's Universality dimension explicitly rewards "demonstrating that the finding generalizes beyond the test cases."

### 1.4 Usefulness to the benchmark community

**Current state**: reasonably actionable for the organizers (§9 gives 3 concrete fix recommendations), and there is real community-interaction evidence (§7.2 cross-references 3 independent public notes; this verification confirmed our Note is published and linked from a post in the challenge discussion area). **The weakest link, and the one place where the highest-weight signal across the four research tracks was not absorbed**: it gives no reusable tool for "other researchers," and no separate, consolidated "what didn't work" section, even though the organizer's pinned post named this out as encouraged.

**Research support**: organizer Owen Vallis's pinned post, verbatim — "We also strongly encourage participants to publish write-ups describing their approach, including ideas that did not work. Those lessons can be just as valuable to the broader research community as the final winning solutions."; Kaggle's official rubric lists "what didn't work" as a mandatory sub-item of the Details section; the analog competition's Image Matching Challenge 2023 first-place has a dedicated numbered subsection "3. Ideas tried but not worked."

### 1.5 Responsible communication

**Current state**: cautious overall — repeatedly limits the covered scope to "does not cover deployed systems," and the payload throughout uses the benign literal test string `SECRET_MARKER` rather than a simulated real credential. But the Note never distinguishes "which disclosed techniques hold only against this sandbox's Python guardrail code (safe)" from "which techniques hold against any real deployed Harmony/gpt-oss model (higher-risk)." §7.3 (line 261) and §7.4 (line 273) quote verbatim two forged Harmony special-token sequences, without any accompanying discussion of the portability of those two strings themselves. **The two risks are unequal**: line 261 sits right next to the document's own admission "Public notebooks later revealed" (the base technique was already disclosed elsewhere, so the marginal risk is limited); line 273 is an original experiment the document itself describes ("Two later variants tested whether...", the 07-21~07-23 work that memory labels "novel"), with no corresponding "already disclosed elsewhere" statement, so its marginal risk should rank ahead.

**Research support**: the closest real precedent, *Policy over Values: Alignment Hacking via CoT Forgery* (an actual $50k Winner), explicitly does not publish the exploit code itself while still describing the mechanism and severity clearly in prose, and requires deleting the reproduction links before sharing — this is a responsible-disclosure standard that judges have actually recognized.

## 3. Concrete recommendations on the T-MAN thread (summary)

**Neither "publish the current draft as-is" nor "keep waiting in silence," but the third path that research angle 4 actually found: minor edits, publish within this week.**

1. **The burden of proof is on The T-MAN, not on us.** The CVE Dispute Policy, COPE's Expression of Concern, and curl maintainer Daniel Stenberg's "DISPUTED, not REJECTED" all independently point to the same threshold: a mere "I don't think so"-style public callout does not constitute evidence requiring the original author to concede; it requires verifiable material like an issue tracker, reproduction code, a specific config, or measured scores. T-MAN has so far only disclosed a technical direction and still not published a score or configuration — this has not reached the evidentiary level of "needing to retract the original claim wholesale."
2. **But complete silence itself has a cost** (the counter-example: Bloomberg's *The Big Hack*). A Working Note is competitively judged, not a rank-independent bank-style bonus, and leaving an absolutizing "strictly loses" claim that is already partly falsified by our own §7.4 data (N=4 forged multi-post scored 87.72, beating the 85.5 single-post baseline) and already publicly called out, sitting unhandled on a published page, only widens the exposure window the longer it drags on.
3. **Attribution ordering is key**: the real reason to publish is "because our own data no longer supports an absolutizing claim," not "because T-MAN says so, so we recant" — the former reads as "doing solid science," the latter reads as "recanting because we got pushed back on." After checking the current diff, §5.5's body already mostly gets this ordering right; what mainly needs adjusting is the Revision banner at the top of the document (see improvement checklist #2). Further: Bruce Schneier's principle (citing Cormac Herley) that "you can prove a system insecure but you cannot conversely prove it secure" shows that any "X is absolutely worse"-style claim is structurally hard to conclusively falsify — the better time is to carry the premises from the initial writing, not to patch after a challenge (improvement checklist #12 turns this into a document-wide writing habit).
4. **For T-MAN's not-yet-confirmed claim, do not write it as confirmed, nor omit it entirely**: the concrete method is to add one dated record sentence in the §5.5 body (improvement checklist #2 gives the specific text), with an effect corresponding to CVE's DISPUTED tag ("an annotation rather than a cancellation") and COPE's Expression of Concern. Once T-MAN actually releases a score/config, we can follow the precedent of the CrowdStrike Ukraine report — narrow only the specific quantitative sub-claim that was challenged, without retracting the core attribution — as a one-time narrow-scope follow-up revision, with no need for another big rewrite.
5. **When responding to T-MAN, keep the tone narrow-scope and on-the-issue-not-the-person** (the IEEE TCRTS author-response guidance, and the APS journals' Comment/Reply mechanism requirement of a "collegial tone, free of polemics"): focus only on the specific claim that was challenged; no need to respond line by line to every sentence in their post.
